The roster problem

Most programmes run mentorship the same way.

There's a roster of volunteers. A founder gets assigned one. They meet once. The second meeting gets rescheduled and then quietly doesn't happen. Nothing is written down. At the end of the year, the programme knows how many sessions were booked and almost nothing about what changed.

This isn't anyone's fault. Good mentors are busy, unpaid and unmanaged, and no individual mentor is responsible for the programme working.

We are. OpenSoch is hired as a firm, not a person.

What we run

Three mandates. Take one or all three.

A

Startup Coaching

Coaching hours, delivered against a roadmap.

Bought by
Programmes by the cohort, companies by the team, and founders one at a time.

We don't run open-ended counselling where a founder shows up and talks about whatever's on top. Every founder moves through a defined sequence, so the hours build on each other instead of resetting each time. A founder who has had four sessions with us is measurably further along a known path — not just four conversations deep.

We work with what's actually in front of a founder in Pakistan: no cap table to speak of, a co-founder problem nobody's naming, a product with three users and a pitch deck written for investors who don't exist here yet. Most coaching frameworks were written for a different market. We translate.

When someone needs a specialist, we bring one in from our expert network rather than pretending general coaching covers it.

B

Programme & Curriculum Design

For institutions that need more than coaching hours.

When it starts
Usually a second-year conversation. Most partners start with coaching, and design follows once we know their people.

There's usually a gap between what a programme offers and what its founders need, and the people running it can see it before anyone else can. We work with them to define a development roadmap and co-design a programme around it — one that holds up when the next cohort, semester or intake arrives.

For universities that means a course or a campus incubator track with the Experiences built into it rather than bolted on. For companies it means an internal innovation programme people volunteer for twice.

The test is repeatability. A programme that works because of who ran it this time isn't a programme.

C

Cohort Systems & Playbooks

The infrastructure underneath both.

Most programmes track their people across a spreadsheet, a WhatsApp group and someone's memory. We build the operating layer: a single record per venture, visible differently to the founder, the programme operator and the funder, so everyone sees what's relevant to them without three separate reporting exercises.

We also write the playbooks — the documented version of how your programme runs, so it survives staff turnover and the end of a semester.

The OpenSoch Coaching Method

Five ideas that show up in almost every engagement.

01Navigate the mess

Uncertainty isn't a phase founders get through on the way to clarity. It's the working condition. Most founder anxiety comes from expecting it to end.

02Work as play

Teams do their most original thinking when the stakes are temporarily lowered. This isn't softness — it's the fastest route to ideas a team can't reach in a status meeting.

03Learn from the losers

Survivor stories are the worst available data. The startups that died are more instructive, and nobody is telling those stories in this ecosystem.

04It takes a village

Most founder problems here are network problems wearing a disguise. Fixing the network fixes more than fixing the pitch deck.

05Know the end game

Founders who haven't thought about how this ends make worse decisions at the start.

Reporting

A written account of what actually happened.

Every institution we work with gets a written account: who engaged, what was covered, what changed, and what we'd recommend for the next cohort or semester.

If you report to a funder, a dean or a board, this is the part that matters most. Publicly funded programmes are asked to show outcomes and are usually left assembling that evidence from attendance sheets after the fact. We produce it as we go, because we're the ones in the room.
